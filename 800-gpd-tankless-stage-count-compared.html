Same 800 GPD, 4 Stages vs. 10: What the Extra Cartridges in a Tankless System Actually Do

By Ilane TallUpdated November 19, 202613 min readResearch-based

We're reader-supported: buying through our links may earn us a commission at no extra cost to you. We compare published specifications, certifications and prices — we don't run a testing lab.

Shop for a tankless reverse osmosis system and you'll land on three units rated at the same 800 gallons a day, priced from $399.99 to $699.00, one listed as a 4-stage system and another as a 10-stage system. The instinct is to read the stage count as a proxy for filtration quality — more stages, more filtering, better water. That instinct is wrong often enough to be dangerous, because GPD rating and stage count measure two completely different things, and a listing title is free to put both numbers next to each other with no implication that one explains the other.

GPD — gallons per day — is a throughput number. It describes how much product water the RO membrane can push out under the manufacturer's assumed feed pressure and temperature, in a 24-hour span. It says nothing about what the water passed through on the way out. A system can be rated at 800 GPD with a single membrane stage and nothing else, or with a membrane plus nine other cartridges ahead of and behind it. The rating is about the pump and the membrane's rejection capacity at a given flow — not about how many things stand between your tap water and that membrane.

Stage count is an architecture number. It counts how many discrete filtration media the water passes through before it reaches your glass: sediment screens, carbon blocks, the membrane itself, post-carbon polish, and — on some systems — a remineralization or pH-adjustment cartridge. Two systems can post the identical 800 GPD throughput while one does it with a bare-minimum stack and the other repeats or splits the same jobs across more cartridges. Neither number predicts the other, and a buying guide that treats "10-stage" as automatically superior to "4-stage" at the same output is reading a spec sheet as if it were a review score.

That's the real question this page answers: at an identical 800 GPD tier, what does each additional stage actually filter, and where does added stage count stop buying you anything? We compare three real listings — the Brio SIMPL at 4 stages, the Frizzlife PD800-TAM4 at 9, and the Waterdrop G3P800-WCK at 10 — against their published specifications and prices, not against invented lab results.

The three stages no tankless 800 GPD system can skip

Before comparing what the extra cartridges do, it helps to know the floor: the minimum job list every RO system — tankless or tank-based, 4-stage or 10-stage — has to complete somewhere in its stack, because reverse osmosis as a process does not work without them.

  • A sediment stage. Particulate — sand, rust, scale fragments — has to be screened out before water reaches anything downstream, or it physically fouls and shortens the life of every cartridge after it. This is the cheapest, least glamorous stage and also the one that protects the most expensive one.
  • A carbon stage (at minimum one). Free chlorine and chloramine in municipal tap water will attack a thin-film composite RO membrane over time. A carbon stage — granular activated carbon, a carbon block, or both — removes that chlorine before the membrane sees it. Skip this and you are not getting "fewer stages for the same result," you're shortening the life of the single most expensive part in the system.
  • The RO membrane itself. The stage that actually performs reverse osmosis — forcing water through a semi-permeable membrane under pressure, rejecting dissolved solids on a size and charge basis. Every GPD rating on every listing in this comparison describes this one component's output.

Three jobs, done somewhere, in some order, with some combination of discrete cartridges. A system that bundles sediment and carbon into a single combo cartridge and calls that "one stage" can do the identical filtration job as a system that splits sediment and carbon into two separate cartridges and calls that "two stages." This is the first reason stage count is a slippery number to compare across brands: it counts cartridges, not jobs, and manufacturers do not agree on how finely to slice the same three-job floor.

9-Stage · 800 GPD · NSF/ANSI 58, 53, 42 & 372 · Alkaline Mineral pH+ · 2:1 pure-to-drain

Frizzlife's listing names four separate NSF/ANSI standards, and each one points at a different job the extra cartridges are likely doing relative to Brio's four. NSF/ANSI 42 governs aesthetic effects — chlorine taste and odor reduction, which is carbon's job. NSF/ANSI 53 governs health effects — reduction of specific contaminants like VOCs and certain heavy metals, which typically means an additional carbon or adsorptive media stage beyond the minimum one. NSF/ANSI 58 is the reverse-osmosis-specific standard covering TDS reduction by the membrane system as a whole. NSF/ANSI 372 only certifies that the materials used are lead-free — it says nothing about contaminant removal. Layer that onto the explicitly named "Alkaline Mineral pH+" stage — a remineralization cartridge that adds minerals back after the membrane strips them out, the same category of stage covered in our filter replacement cost breakdown — and five of Frizzlife's extra cartridges over Brio's four are accounted for by name: an added carbon/adsorption stage for the 53 certification, a dedicated post-membrane polish, and a multi-cartridge alkaline mineral stage, which alkaline systems commonly split into two or three separate media cartridges rather than one. The listing's own 2:1 pure-to-drain ratio is also a meaningfully better efficiency number than an older 3:1 or 4:1 design, a distinction we cover in more depth in our noise and efficiency comparison of non-electric vs. tankless systems.

Is Waterdrop's tenth stage a real upgrade, or diminishing returns?

Here is the honest answer, not the one that makes a tidier narrative: based on what Waterdrop's own listing discloses, there isn't enough information to say definitively. The listing commits to a stage count and a drain ratio and stops there. That absence is itself informative, and it is the central argument of this article — a bigger number on a listing title is not the same thing as a disclosed benefit.

What we can say from general RO system architecture, without inventing specifics for this particular unit, is where a system typically gains stages once it has already covered sediment, carbon, membrane and post-carbon. The most common places are: a second, finer sediment pre-filter ahead of the first (protecting the membrane harder, which matters more on sediment-heavy or well-water supplies than on treated municipal water); a duplicated or split carbon stage (two carbon media types instead of one, for broader chlorine-and-taste coverage); or a second post-membrane polish stage. Any of those is a legitimate, if incremental, upgrade over a 9-stage design that already covers the same job list once. None of them is a different kind of contaminant removal than the membrane itself is already doing — the RO membrane remains the stage doing the heavy rejection work in every one of these three systems, at 800 GPD apiece.

The practical takeaway: a 10-stage system is not inherently filtering "more thoroughly" than a 9-stage one in a way you should pay a premium for unless the listing tells you what fills the stages the cheaper competitor lacks. On price alone, Waterdrop's unit costs $339.01 more than Frizzlife's — and runs a worse 3:1 drain ratio, which factors into ongoing water cost the same way filter cartridges factor into ongoing filter cost. One stage of unexplained difference is a thin justification for that gap on its own; it more plausibly reflects brand positioning, the smart faucet, and build quality that isn't captured by a stage count at all.

None of those are illegitimate reasons for a product to cost more — but they are not the same reason as "ten stages filter better than nine," and a shopper comparing on stage count alone will miss the distinction entirely.

How to read a tankless listing's stage breakdown instead of trusting the number

Three checks, in the order we'd apply them to any 800 GPD listing — tankless or otherwise.

1. Look for named standards, not just a stage count. Frizzlife's listing naming NSF/ANSI 58, 53, 42 and 372 tells you more about what the system actually does than "9-Stage" does on its own — because each standard is a specific, checkable claim about a specific category of removal, not a cartridge tally. A listing with a high stage count and zero named standards, like Waterdrop's G3P800-WCK here, has told you less, not more, regardless of which number is bigger.

2. Ask what job each additional stage over the three-stage floor is doing. Sediment, carbon and membrane are the floor covered in the section above. Every stage past that should map to something: a named remineralization cartridge, a second carbon media for broader coverage, a finer pre-filter. If a listing's extra stages don't map to anything you can name, that is useful information — it tells you the number may be doing more marketing work than filtration work. We walk through the same discipline applied to running costs, where the identical logic applies to cartridge pricing, in our bulk replacement filter pack breakdown.

3. Price the drain ratio and the warranty alongside the stage count, not instead of it. A worse pure-to-drain ratio is a real, ongoing cost that a stage count says nothing about, and it is exactly the kind of spec that gets buried under a bigger, more marketable number. The same goes for what happens if a cartridge fails early — our warranty coverage comparison breaks down what "1-year limited" actually covers across brands, which matters more to total cost of ownership than whether a system has nine stages or ten.

None of this means buy the cheapest or the most expensive system in a stage-count race. It means treat the stage count the way you'd treat the GPD rating itself: one real number, among several, that only tells you something useful once you know what it is actually measuring.

FAQ

Questions people ask about RO stage counts

Does a higher stage count mean better filtration?

Not reliably. Stage count tallies discrete cartridges, not filtration jobs, and manufacturers split the same jobs into different numbers of cartridges. A 4-stage system covering sediment, carbon, membrane and post-carbon can do the identical core job as a 9-stage system that splits carbon into two media and adds a multi-cartridge remineralization stage. What matters is what each stage past the three-stage floor is named to do — not the total count.

What are the minimum stages a reverse osmosis system needs?

Three jobs, regardless of how many cartridges a listing counts them as: a sediment stage to remove particulate, at least one carbon stage to protect the membrane from chlorine and improve taste, and the RO membrane itself, which performs the actual reverse osmosis filtration. Every system in this comparison, from 4-stage to 10-stage, covers this floor; the stages above it are where designs diverge.

Is GPD rating related to stage count?

No — they measure different things. GPD (gallons per day) is a throughput figure describing how much water the membrane can produce under the manufacturer's assumed conditions. Stage count describes how many filtration cartridges the water passes through on the way there. Brio, Frizzlife and Waterdrop's systems compared here are all rated at 800 GPD with stage counts of 4, 9 and 10 respectively, which is the clearest possible demonstration that the two numbers are independent.

Why does Waterdrop's 10-stage system cost so much more than Frizzlife's 9-stage system?

The $339.01 price gap between them is larger than a single added stage plausibly explains on filtration grounds alone, especially since Frizzlife's five additional stages over Brio's four cost only about $22 each. The difference more likely reflects the smart faucet, overall build and brand positioning rather than one extra filtration stage. Waterdrop's listing also runs a 3:1 pure-to-drain ratio, worse than Frizzlife's 2:1, which is a real ongoing cost a stage count doesn't capture.
