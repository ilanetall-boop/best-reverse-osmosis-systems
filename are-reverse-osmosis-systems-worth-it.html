The real comparison

Name the thing you'd actually be replacing

A reverse osmosis system never competes against an abstract idea of pure water. It competes against whatever you already do at four o'clock on a Tuesday when you want a cold glass of something. There are three honest alternatives, and RO has to beat the one you use — not the worst of the three.

Bottled or delivered water. The alternative RO wins against most often, because it's the only one with a running cost as large as its own — and the one people underestimate: the case in the trunk, the empties by the door, the 11pm discovery that you're out. If this is you, skip to the arithmetic; the answer is probably yes, and the only question is which route.

A carbon pitcher or faucet filter. Be fair to this one, because the industry rarely is. For twenty to forty dollars plus a cartridge every couple of months, activated carbon removes chlorine taste and odour, and certified models are rated for specific contaminants too. On a great many US municipal supplies that is genuinely enough: the water is already treated and tested against federal limits, and the usual complaint is that it tastes of the chlorine that made it safe.

Where carbon stops is dissolved solids. It's a screen and a sponge, not a membrane, so it can't meaningfully reduce nitrate, fluoride, arsenic, sodium or the TDS load that makes some tap water taste chalky — which is why RO exists, and why NSF/ANSI 58, written for reverse osmosis systems and their contaminant reduction, isn't what a pitcher is certified against. If your problem is taste, carbon is the cheap answer. If it's what's dissolved in the water, carbon isn't an answer at all.

Doing nothing. We run an affiliate site and we'll still say it: if your utility's annual water quality report comes back clean, your kettle isn't furring up and you're happy drinking from the tap, doing nothing is legitimate and costs nothing.

The arithmetic

The payback period, with the assumptions showing

Every payback figure you've seen was built on assumptions somebody chose not to show you. Here are ours.

  • A household drinking and cooking with 10 gallons a week — 520 gallons a year. A family of four, or two people who drink a lot of tea.
  • Bottled water at $1.20 a gallon, the supermarket jug price. Cases of small bottles run closer to $1.85 a gallon.
  • Combined water and sewer at $0.015 a gallon, for the drain math below. Check your own bill — US rates vary by a factor of several between cities.
  • No plumber. If you hire one, add the call-out and push every break-even out by a month or two.

At those assumptions, bottled water costs $624 a year, about $52 a month. That is the number RO has to beat.

Take the cheapest certified route first. The iSpring RCC7AK-BN is $198.76 up front and uses universal 10-inch by 2.5-inch cartridges, so its consumables are commodity parts, not a proprietary subscription: the F9K one-year set is $69.24 for nine. The membrane isn't in that box — it lasts two to three years and is bought separately — so add an allowance of roughly $25 a year — an allowance, note, not a quoted price. Running cost: about $94 a year, $7.85 a month.

Break-even is where what you've spent on the system equals what you'd have spent on bottles: $198.76 ÷ ($52.00 − $7.85) = 4.5 months, after which it saves roughly $530 a year.

The tankless route costs more. The Waterdrop G2P600 is $299.00, and its own cartridge price isn't in the data set we work from; the closest we have a quoted price for is the G3-N1CF front filter at $29.99, rated for six months or 550 gallons — which is where the planning figure of about $60 a year comes from. At $5 a month: $299.00 ÷ ($52.00 − $5.00) = 6.4 months.

Both land inside a year, which is where most buying guides stop. Change one assumption at a time and it moves.

  • A couple drinking 4 gallons a week: bottled drops to $250 a year, and the iSpring's break-even stretches to about 15 months, the Waterdrop to 19. Fine over a decade — but "pays for itself before Christmas" is gone.
  • Cases of 16.9 oz bottles at $1.85 a gallon: the baseline jumps to $962 a year and the iSpring pays back in under three months.
  • You drink tap water for free today: there is no payback. Not a long one — none. The system is a purchase, not an investment.

What moves the answer most is not the price of the system or the filters. It's the price of what you're replacing, times how much of it you drink: between a tap drinker and a household buying a case a week, the same $299.00 system swings from never paying back to paid off by spring. None of it is a promise — prices move, and a system abandoned in year two has a payback of never. This is arithmetic on stated assumptions, not a forecast.

Ownership

The running costs people leave out

The sticker price is the smaller half of the decision. Four things keep costing after the box is in the cabinet.

Cartridges, every six to twelve months. The gap between platforms is wide: the APEC Essence series takes a $25.46 three-pack twice a year, iSpring's full one-year set is $69.24, and proprietary twist-ins cost more per change at $29.99 apiece for the Waterdrop front filter. Over ten years that difference is larger than the difference between the systems themselves — which is why we keep a separate breakdown of filter replacement costs.

The membrane, every two to three years. The part that actually does reverse osmosis is almost never in the annual filter set — iSpring's F9K pack says so explicitly. For a sense of what one is worth inside a bundle, iSpring's F6K75 six-month set including a 75 GPD membrane is $87.69, against $55.09 for the five-stage F5-75 pack.

Electricity, if the system is pumped. Tankless systems generally need a booster pump and an outlet; the Waterdrop G3P600 listing states it outright. Nobody publishes an annual kWh figure and we don't run a testing lab, so we won't invent one — the pump runs only while you're drawing water, which makes this a few dollars a year, not a deciding line item. The Frizzlife M800 has no cord at all.

And the water that goes down the drain — big enough for its own section, smaller than the internet thinks. One one-off gets forgotten too: the faucet hole, free if your sink has a spare knockout, a job for a diamond bit if the counter is granite (our installation guide covers it).

Quantified

The water you send to the drain, in gallons and in dollars

Reverse osmosis rinses the rejected minerals to the drain, so some water is lost. The ratio is a published specification and varies enormously. For the same 520-gallon-a-year household:

  • An older 3:1 system — three gallons to drain per gallon produced — sends about 1,560 gallons a year down the pipe. Some legacy systems are 4:1.
  • A 2:1 tankless unit like the Waterdrop G2P600, whose listing puts it at one cup of waste per two cups of pure water, sends about 260 gallons a year.
  • The Frizzlife M800, at a claimed 4:1 pure-to-drain — the ratio running in your favour rather than against it — sends about 130 gallons a year.

At $0.015 a gallon for combined water and sewer, that's $23.40 a year for the old system, $3.90 for the 2:1 tankless and $1.95 for the Frizzlife. The spread between worst and best is about twenty-one dollars a year.

We'll say the obvious thing: that is a small number and it won't decide your purchase. Where it stops being small is elsewhere — in a drought-restricted region the gallons matter more than the dollars, and on a well with a septic field that volume goes into a system you maintain yourself. A wasteful system on marginal pressure also produces slowly, which people experience as a bad system long before a bill.

One reassurance, since it comes up constantly: the drain water isn't toxic. It's your own tap water with the rejected minerals concentrated in it — Waterdrop's listing suggests mopping floors or washing a car with it, which is practical on a countertop unit and theoretical on a plumbed one.

The yes cases

When an RO system clearly earns its place

You're on well water. Nobody is treating it and nobody is testing it but you, and what turns up — nitrate near farmland, iron, hardness, a high dissolved-solids load — is exactly the class of problem carbon cannot touch. Test at a certified lab first, then buy for the results.

Your municipal supply is safe but high-TDS. Plenty of US supplies meet every federal limit and still measure several hundred parts per million: water that tastes chalky, spots glasses and furs a kettle in a month. iSpring publishes 93–98% TDS reduction for the RCC7AK-BN.

You have a contaminant issue you've actually had tested. Not read about — tested, in your utility's annual report or by a certified lab. Then buy on certification and read it precisely, because the labels are easy to blur: NSF/ANSI 58 certifies contaminant and TDS reduction performance, NSF/ANSI 372 certifies only that the materials are lead-free, and WQA is a different certifying body rather than a different standard. The iSpring is certified as a whole system to NSF/ANSI 58; the Frizzlife M800 against 58 and 372; the Waterdrop G2P600 carries 372 alone.

You keep an aquarium or a serious espresso machine. Reef keepers want water with nothing in it so they can dose exactly what they intend; espresso people want low scale in a boiler that costs more than the RO system. One nuance for coffee: extraction wants some mineral content, so the usual answer is RO plus a remineralization stage — which is what the alkaline stages here are for, and what our guide to whether RO removes minerals covers.

You currently buy bottled water by the case. The clearest yes on the page: you already pay a running cost, and RO replaces it with a smaller one.

The no cases

When it isn't worth it — and we'd rather say so than sell you one

Plenty of households that land on a page like this shouldn't buy one. Being useful means naming them.

You rent, the lease is short, and there's no counter space either. Drilling a faucet hole in someone else's countertop isn't a small decision, and a system you'll uninstall in eight months has to earn its keep in eight. The countertop route exists for this: the Bluevua RO100ROPOT-Lite is $219.00, needs no plumbing and leaves with you — our countertop versus under-sink comparison covers the trade. But if you can't spare the footprint of a coffee maker, buy nothing rather than something that ends up in a closet.

You're on good municipal water and your only complaint is chlorine taste. A carbon pitcher fixes that for around thirty dollars. Spending $299.00 and an afternoon under the sink to fix the taste of chlorine is buying a bulldozer to plant a shrub.

You have no space and no outlet, and the compromise would be permanent. A system wedged into a bad spot is one you resent every time you reach past it for the dish soap, and that outlives the novelty of the water by years. The Frizzlife M800 solves the outlet problem but not the space one — it's still a 19.7-inch-tall box. Measure the cabinet, including behind the P-trap, before anything else; our tankless versus tank comparison is a guide to which compromise you can live with.

You're hoping it will fix a health problem. This is general consumer information, not medical advice, and we'd rather be blunt than vague: an RO system is a water quality device. It reduces dissolved contaminants, which is measurable. It is not a treatment, and buying one to address a symptom is the single purchase on this page we'd talk you out of. A health concern is a conversation with a clinician; a contaminant concern is a lab test first.

You know you won't change the filters. A system with a two-year-old sediment cartridge is worse than none at all. If "never thinking about water" means "never maintaining it", bottled water is the more honest purchase.

Off the spreadsheet

The reasons owners keep them that have nothing to do with money

If payback were the only argument, nobody with cheap, pleasant municipal water would own one — and plenty do. What comes up again and again in owner reviews isn't purity. It's that the errand disappeared: no cases in the trunk, no empties by the door, no running out at eleven at night. Ice tastes of nothing, coffee changes once the chlorine is gone, and after the first filter change the whole thing recedes into the background — which is the actual product. Whether that's worth $198.76 is a preference, not a calculation, and preferences are allowed to win, as long as you're not calling it an investment when it's a convenience.

The verdict

So: worth it for whom?

  • You buy bottled water, there are three or more of you, and you own the kitchen. Yes, comfortably: the iSpring RCC7AK-BN at $198.76, the cheapest path to an NSF/ANSI 58-certified system with commodity cartridges.
  • You buy bottled water, the cabinet is tight, there's an outlet. Yes, with the tankless premium: the Waterdrop G2P600 at $299.00, if you accept NSF/ANSI 372 for materials in place of a 58 performance certification.
  • Same, but no outlet under the sink. The Frizzlife M800 at $299.99, if the 19.7-inch height fits and your pressure is healthy.
  • You rent, or you can't plumb anything. The Bluevua RO100ROPOT-Lite at $219.00 — or nothing, if you can't spare the counter.
  • Good municipal water, and the only issue is taste. No. Buy a carbon pitcher and spend the difference on something you'll enjoy more.
  • You drink tap water, you're happy, nothing has tested badly. No, and no cost math will change that. Doing nothing remains undefeated on price.

FAQ

The questions behind the question

Is a reverse osmosis system cheaper than bottled water?

For most households that currently buy bottled water, yes, by a wide margin. On our stated assumptions — 10 gallons a week at $1.20 a gallon — bottled costs about $624 a year, while an iSpring RCC7AK-BN at $198.76 runs roughly $94 a year afterwards including a membrane allowance. Buy cases of small bottles and the gap is wider; drink four gallons a week and it narrows sharply.

How long does an RO system take to pay for itself?

On the same assumptions, about 4.5 months for the $198.76 iSpring RCC7AK-BN and about 6.4 months for the $299.00 Waterdrop G2P600. Those are estimates on published assumptions, not promises: what moves them most is how much water you drink and what you pay per gallon today. If you drink tap water, there is no payback at all.

Is the water an RO system sends down the drain a real problem?

In dollars, usually not. A household making 520 gallons of RO water a year sends about 1,560 gallons to the drain on an older 3:1 system, 260 on a 2:1 tankless unit and about 130 on the Frizzlife M800's claimed 4:1 ratio. At $0.015 a gallon for combined water and sewer that is roughly $23, $4 and $2 a year. It matters more where water is scarce, or on a well with a septic field, than it does on the bill.

Who should not buy a reverse osmosis system?

Anyone on good municipal water whose only complaint is chlorine taste, since a carbon pitcher fixes that for a fraction of the money. Renters on a short lease with no counter space for even a countertop unit. Households where the system would make the kitchen permanently worse. And anyone hoping filtration will resolve a health problem: that is general consumer information rather than medical advice, and an RO system is a water quality device, not a treatment.
