Does reverse osmosis remove minerals? What that actually means

By Ilane TallUpdated August 13, 202612 min readResearch-based

We're reader-supported: buying through our links may earn us a commission at no extra cost to you. We compare published specifications, certifications and prices — we don't run a testing lab.

Ask this in two different places and you get two confident, opposite answers. In one corner, reverse osmosis produces dead water that leaches minerals out of your body. In the other, the minerals in tap water are nutritionally irrelevant and nobody should spend a second on them. Both camps usually have something to sell.

The factual part is short and undisputed: yes, RO removes most of the dissolved minerals from your water along with the dissolved contaminants, because the membrane cannot tell them apart. Everything worth discussing happens after that sentence.

One thing plainly, up front: everything here that touches on health is general consumer information, not medical advice. We don't know your water, your diet or your history, and if you have a specific medical reason to care about mineral intake — a kidney condition, a prescribed diet, an infant on formula — that's a conversation with your doctor, not with a buying guide.

How the membrane decides what stays and what goes

An RO membrane is a semi-permeable film with pores in the neighborhood of 0.0001 micron — both iSpring and Bluevua publish that figure. Household pressure pushes water against it. Water molecules pass. Almost everything dissolved in the water does not, because it is either too large for the pore or carries an electrical charge the membrane rejects, and the rejected fraction goes down the drain.

Here is the part that answers the question. “Dissolved solids” is not a category of bad things; it is a category of things dissolved in water. Calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium, bicarbonate and sulfate are dissolved solids. So are lead, arsenic, fluoride, nitrate and chromium. A membrane that rejects one rejects the other, and manufacturers are candid about it if you read past the headline. Waterdrop's own description of the G3P600 lists what it reduces in one breath — TDS, chromium, PFAS, radium, fluoride, arsenic salt, iron, calcium, chloride, chlorine — with calcium sitting between iron and chloride. That isn't sloppy copywriting; it's the mechanism described accurately. The property that removes lead and PFAS is the property that removes calcium and magnesium.

So, unambiguously: there is no membrane that keeps the good ones and rejects the bad ones. Selectivity by size and charge does not map onto selectivity by virtue. Every system marketed as mineral RO or alkaline RO strips the water first and adds minerals back afterwards, in a separate stage downstream of the membrane. That turns a confusing category into a plain hardware question: do you want the extra stage, and what does it cost? Every number below comes from a specification sheet or is arithmetic we show our work for.

What that does — and doesn't — actually mean

Drinking water is a minor contributor to mineral intake for most people eating an ordinary diet. Calcium comes overwhelmingly from dairy, greens, legumes and fortified foods; magnesium from nuts, seeds, whole grains and greens. Water contributes something, and more than nothing in hard-water regions, but it is small next to a plate of food. Scale is exactly what both sides of this argument leave out.

We're deliberately not handing you a percentage, because the honest answer depends on two things only you know: how mineralized your supply is, and what you eat. A household on a soft municipal supply loses little to the membrane, because there was little there. A household on a hard well loses a lot — and that same hardness has been scaling the kettle and spotting the glasses. Any guide quoting one confident number for how much calcium RO costs you per day has invented it.

Two things follow. The framing that survives scrutiny is taste and preference first, nutrition second: almost nobody adds a mineral stage because of a blood test, they add it because the water tastes like nothing. And don't let anyone, us included, sell you certainty in either direction. It is not established that mineral-free drinking water harms a well-fed adult, nor that mineral content in water is meaningless for everyone. What is established is that the stage costs about thirty dollars and is reversible.

Why RO water tastes flat, and why that's the real reason people add minerals

This is the complaint that genuinely shows up after an install, and it isn't imaginary. What you taste in water is mostly what's dissolved in it: bicarbonate gives water body, calcium and magnesium add a mineral edge, sulfate reads as crisp or in quantity slightly bitter. Take a supply running at 250 ppm of dissolved solids and drop it into the low double digits — iSpring publishes 93% to 98% TDS reduction for the RCC7AK-BN, and up to 97% for its 75 GPD membranes under NSF/ANSI 58 test conditions — and you haven't only removed the chlorine and the metals. You've removed most of the flavor. Low TDS carries less taste, and that is the whole explanation.

People describe the result as flat, empty, or like drinking nothing. That last one is sometimes a compliment: coming off a heavily chlorinated supply, or a well with a sulfur note, like nothing is exactly the upgrade you paid for. Coming off a decent, moderately hard supply you were quietly fond of, it's a loss — and you notice it in tea, in coffee, and in a glass of cold water at two in the morning. This, not nutrition, is why most mineral cartridges get bought.

One caveat before you judge: fresh carbon sheds fines and a taste of its own for the first few gallons, so do the flush your installation manual asks for and give it two days.

The theme that repeats across owner reviews of remineralization cartridges: the water was obviously cleaner and obviously less interesting, and the people who added a mineral stage decided within the first month or never bothered at all.

pH, alkaline water, and where the specification sheet stops

RO water tends to be slightly acidic, and iSpring says so in its own documentation rather than leaving you to discover it: a standard 5-stage RO system produces slightly acidic water with a pH of 7.0 or below. The bicarbonate that buffers your tap water is a dissolved solid, so the membrane takes it out; dissolved carbon dioxide, being a gas, passes straight through and forms a little carbonic acid with nothing left to neutralize it.

An alkaline cartridge is mechanically unglamorous, which is a point in its favor. It's a small housing packed with mineral media — calcite, mineral stone or ceramic granules. Purified water runs through the bed on its way to the faucet and dissolves a small, self-limiting quantity of it; mineral content goes up and pH follows. Waterdrop, iSpring, APEC and Frizzlife each describe theirs as restoring calcium and magnesium, with sodium, potassium or zinc depending on the media. Two consequences: the bed dissolves, so it is consumed, which is why these cartridges carry a service life in months and gallons rather than years. And the effect is bounded — you're dissolving a trace of mineral into water, not reconstituting a mountain spring.

Which brings us to the part we won't repeat as fact. Waterdrop's listing for the MNR35 states that drinking its mineral water will, in the long run, help avoid chronic diseases, slow the aging process and improve digestion and immunity. That's manufacturer copy, we have no way to verify it, and we're not going to launder it into an editorial claim. What is checkable is the boring half: the cartridge costs $29.99, is rated for 9 to 12 months or 1,100 gallons, and fits 1/4-inch and 3/8-inch tubing. Buy it for taste and a measurable pH shift; anything past that is marketing, not specification.

What a mineral stage costs to keep running

As a standalone cartridge. The MNR35 is $29.99, rated 9 to 12 months or 1,100 gallons, whichever comes first. On Waterdrop's recommended nine-month schedule that's $29.99 divided by 0.75 of a year, or $39.99 annually; stretched to twelve months it's $29.99. Call it $30 to $40 a year, or about $0.027 per gallon at the rated life. A household drawing two gallons a day uses 730 gallons a year, well inside the 1,100-gallon rating, so the calendar limit is the one you'll hit; a heavier household at four gallons a day hits the gallon rating at around nine months instead. Either way, budget one cartridge a year.

Inside a system. Here the mineral stage nearly disappears as a line item. The iSpring F9K year-set is $69.24 for nine cartridges, two of which are the alkaline stage — you were buying the sediment, carbon and post-carbon filters regardless, so the mineral capability rides along inside a bill you already had. APEC works differently, and it pays to know before you budget: its FILTER-SET-ES pre-filter set is $25.46 for six months and contains one sediment filter and two carbon blocks, with the calcium cartridge bought separately.

Where it sits in the yearly bill. Small. RO running costs are dominated by pre-filters two or three times a year and, every two to three years, the membrane. A mineral stage moves your annual number by tens of dollars, not hundreds, and it should not decide which system you buy. Who should skip a mineral stage, and who really shouldn't

Skip it if you want low dissolved solids on purpose. Reef and planted-aquarium keepers are the clearest case: the point of RO or RO/DI water in that hobby is a blank slate, remineralized afterwards to a controlled recipe the keeper chooses and measures. An uncontrolled mineral bed upstream of that is counterproductive — you're adding an unknown you then have to correct for.

Espresso is more nuanced than the internet suggests. Machine owners run RO water to keep scale out of a boiler, a real and expensive problem. But zero isn't the target either: water with essentially nothing dissolved in it extracts poorly and is unkind to metal components, which is why people who care about this blend RO water with a measured mineral concentrate to hit a recipe. An inline cartridge you can't dose or verify is the wrong instrument for that. The same logic covers a steam oven fed from the RO faucet, and anyone whose original complaint was hardness — adding minerals back moves in the direction you paid to move away from.

Now the people who should. The largest group by a distance: households who installed a system, drank the water, and quietly missed the taste of their own tap. That's not a nutritional complaint and it doesn't need to dress up as one — it's a thirty-dollar fix for something you notice several times a day. Close behind are people who were buying bottled mineral water for the taste and expected RO to replace it; without a mineral stage it doesn't. Then a group nobody talks about: people who install RO and then drink less water, because they don't enjoy it any more. If that's your kitchen, the mineral stage isn't a nicety — it's what makes the system you already paid for get used.

If you can't decide, the sequencing is easy: install without, live with the water for a month, add the cartridge afterwards if you miss it. It's a $29.99 experiment, not a fork in the road — and it's why we wouldn't pay a large premium for a system on the strength of its mineral stage alone. How to judge this for yourself: the TDS meter, and its limits

A TDS pen is a cheap conductivity meter, and it's the one measurement in this article you can make yourself. Take three readings: your cold tap, the RO faucet, and — if you've fitted a mineral stage — the RO faucet again with that cartridge in line. The drop from the first to the second is the membrane working; a fall from a few hundred ppm to the low double digits is the normal picture. The rise to the third is your mineral cartridge dosing, and it should be a modest, consistent climb rather than a return toward tap levels. The Waterdrop G3P600 and Frizzlife M800 save you the errand by displaying live TDS on the faucet.

Now the part most people get wrong, and it matters more than the readings. TDS is one aggregate number derived from electrical conductivity. It tells you how much dissolved material is present and nothing about which. Three hundred ppm of calcium bicarbonate from a limestone aquifer and three hundred ppm that happens to include sodium, nitrate and a little arsenic read identically on the pen. A low number is not a certificate of safety; a high number is not evidence of danger. The meter is also blind to whole categories of things people buy RO for: PFAS are measured at parts per trillion, far below what a conductivity meter can resolve, and bacteria and cysts are not dissolved solids at all. That's why we lean on NSF/ANSI 58 rather than a pen reading — the certification means an independent body verified a specific reduction claim, whereas the pen means a needle moved.

Where the meter earns its place is trend, not truth. Log the RO reading every couple of months against a stable tap reading. When it starts creeping upward, the membrane is aging — a maintenance signal no calendar reminder can give you.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask

Does reverse osmosis remove all the minerals from water?

Not all, but most. Manufacturers publish a reduction percentage rather than total removal: iSpring rates the RCC7AK-BN at 93% to 98% TDS reduction, and its 75 GPD membranes at up to 97% under NSF/ANSI 58 test conditions. A supply arriving at 250 ppm therefore typically leaves the faucet somewhere in the single digits to the low twenties rather than at zero. What remains is a trace of whatever was in the water to begin with.

Is reverse osmosis water bad for you?

That is not a question a buying guide should answer as if it were a health authority, and we won't. What we can offer is the practical framing: drinking water is a minor contributor to mineral intake compared with food for most people on an ordinary diet, the flat taste is the complaint owners actually report after an install, and a remineralization stage costs about $30 if you want the minerals and the flavor back. Anything specific to your own health belongs with your doctor rather than with us.

Can I add a remineralization filter to any RO system?

In practice, yes for almost any under-sink system. The Waterdrop MNR35 is built for 1/4-inch and 3/8-inch quick-connect tubing, which is what nearly every under-sink RO system sold in the US uses, and it fits inline between the final stage and the faucet regardless of brand. The exceptions are sealed countertop units, where you cannot reach the internal plumbing, and hot-water lines, which Waterdrop explicitly says not to connect it to.

Will a mineral filter raise my TDS reading, and is that a problem?

Yes it will, and no it is not. The cartridge works by dissolving mineral into the water, so the meter climbs — that rise is the cartridge doing its job, and a stable rise is how you confirm it is still dosing. What matters is knowing which reading you are taking. A measurement at the faucet downstream of the mineral stage reflects purified water plus added mineral, not membrane performance. To judge the membrane itself you need a reading taken before the mineral stage.
