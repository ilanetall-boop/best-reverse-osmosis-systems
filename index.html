Before you buy

Four things that decide whether you'll be happy

01

Cabinet space

A classic system is three housings plus a pressure tank — most of a cabinet. Tankless units are one slim box. Measure the space, including the depth behind your P-trap, before anything else.

02

Which certification

NSF/ANSI 372 only means lead-free materials. NSF/ANSI 58 is the one that certifies the system actually reduces dissolved solids. They are not interchangeable.

03

Drain ratio

RO sends part of your tap water down the drain. Older systems run 3:1 or 4:1 against you; the modern picks here are 2:1, and one is 4:1 in your favour. On a metered bill it adds up.

04

Filter economics

Cartridges are the real cost of ownership. Standard 10"×2.5" housings take generic sets from about $25; proprietary twist-in filters are quicker to change and cost more.

FAQ

Common questions

Do I need a plumber to install one?

Usually not. An under-sink system connects to the cold-water line with a self-piercing or compression feed valve, drops a drain saddle onto the waste pipe, and needs a hole for the dedicated faucet — most sinks already have a spare hole for a sprayer or soap dispenser. Budget two to three hours the first time. Where people do call a plumber is when the sink has no spare hole and the countertop is granite or quartz, which needs a diamond bit and a steady hand.

How much water does reverse osmosis waste?

Some — it is how the process works. The membrane rinses rejected minerals down the drain, and the ratio is a published specification. Older systems run 3:1 or 4:1 against you; the tankless picks here are 2:1, and the Frizzlife M800 claims 4:1 in your favour. On a metered supply the difference between a 4:1 waste ratio and 2:1 is real money over a year, so it belongs in the comparison.

Does RO strip the minerals out of my water?

Yes, largely — that is the same mechanism that removes dissolved contaminants. Whether it matters is a separate question: drinking water is a small contributor to mineral intake compared with food. If you want the taste of mineral water back, several picks here add calcium and magnesium after the membrane, and a standalone remineralization cartridge can be added to almost any system for around $30. This is general information, not medical advice.

How often do the filters need changing, and what does it cost?

Sediment and carbon stages run six to twelve months; the RO membrane typically two to three years. In practice that is roughly $25 to $70 a year for standard-housing systems and about $60 a year for proprietary twist-in cartridges. We break the numbers down system by system in our filter replacement cost guide.

Tank or tankless — which should I get?

Tankless if cabinet space is tight and there's an outlet: you get faster flow, nothing stagnating in a bladder, and a slimmer box. Tank if you want the cheapest running costs and universal cartridges, or if you have no power under the sink and don't want to pay the non-electric premium. Our tankless vs tank comparison works through both cases.
