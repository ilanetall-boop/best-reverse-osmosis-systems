Reverse osmosis for aquariums and reef tanks: why plain beats alkaline

Type "reverse osmosis" into a search bar and most of the results are drinking-water buyers trying to solve the same problem: tap water carries dissolved solids they'd rather not drink. Aquarium keepers show up in the same search for a related reason, but the water leaving their RO system is never going in a glass. It's going into a sump, a mixing barrel, or a reservoir that feeds an auto top-off — and what they're actually buying is a blank slate.

Municipal tap water is formulated to be safe for people, not neutral for a closed aquatic system. Treatment plants commonly add chloramine as a disinfectant, and many add phosphate-based corrosion inhibitors to protect old pipes — a sensible public-health choice that happens to double as fertilizer for the nuisance algae and diatoms every reef and planted-tank keeper is fighting. Dissolved silica, common in some municipal supplies and most well water, feeds diatoms the same way. Copper picked up from household plumbing is safe at levels regulators care about and toxic to invertebrates — shrimp, snails, many corals — at a fraction of that concentration. None of this shows up as a taste problem; it shows up weeks later as a tank full of algae or a crashed invertebrate population, with no obvious cause unless you know to suspect the water source.

That's the real reason RO shows up in aquarium keeping: not purity for its own sake, but a known, repeatable starting point. A reef keeper mixes synthetic sea salt into RO (or RO/DI) water because the salt manufacturer calibrated the mix assuming zero ppm feedwater — add tap water's unpredictable baseline of calcium, phosphate and nitrate underneath that salt, and the calcium, alkalinity and magnesium numbers on the bag are no longer the numbers you'll actually get. Planted-tank keepers dosing a fertilizer line have the same problem in reverse: a dosing schedule built around a target GH and KH only holds if the water starts at a known baseline, not whatever the utility put through the pipes that week. RO subtracts the one variable nobody can control from the tap, so everything added afterward is deliberate.

Why a plain system beats an alkaline one here — the opposite of the usual advice

Everywhere else on this site, when we talk about choosing an RO system for drinking water, an alkaline or remineralizing stage is a point in a system's favor. Stripping water down to near-zero dissolved solids can leave it tasting flat next to tap water, so systems like the alkaline picks in our main under-sink roundup add a mineral cartridge after the membrane that puts calcium and magnesium back in, specifically to make RO water taste more like what people are used to drinking.

An aquarium is the mirror image of that use case, and it's worth saying plainly because it contradicts the drinking-water advice on the rest of this site: for a reef or planted tank, that same alkaline stage is a defect, not a feature. You are not drinking this water. You are using it as the solvent for a different, deliberate chemistry you add yourself afterward — synthetic sea salt mixed to a specific calcium-to-alkalinity-to-magnesium ratio, or a fertilizer line dosed to a target GH and KH. An alkaline cartridge reintroduces calcium and magnesium at whatever ratio the filter manufacturer judged best for human taste. That ratio has nothing to do with your salt mix or fertilizer schedule, and it isn't printed anywhere you'd see it before the water's already in the tank — an unmeasured mineral input sitting underneath the measured one you actually wanted to control. The entire point of running RO for an aquarium was to remove exactly that kind of unknown.

All three systems in this guide are plain designs with no alkaline or remineralization stage — a 5-stage and a 7-stage system built around filtration alone, which is the right starting point here regardless of brand. If you already own one of the alkaline systems from our main roundup, bought before the tank arrived, the fix usually isn't replacing the whole unit: on most alkaline RO systems the mineral cartridge sits in its own separate housing, and bypassing that one stage returns the system to a plain configuration. Check your specific listing or manual before assuming that's true of yours — some remineralization stages are integrated differently, and we don't have that detail for every alkaline system on the market.

GPD math: what a water change actually needs

GPD — gallons per day — is the standard output rating on every RO system's box, and like most specs in this category it's measured under favorable conditions: higher incoming pressure and warmer feed water than a lot of real houses deliver. Treat the printed number as a ceiling you're working toward, not a guarantee of what you'll get the first week.

Of the three systems here, the Express Water RO5DX is rated at 50 GPD, the iSpring RCC7-BN at 75 GPD, and the VonWater 600GPD at up to 600 GPD. The VonWater's listing also publishes an instantaneous flow rate — 0.42 GPM, about 25 gallons an hour — which is how a tankless design reaches a number that high: there's no storage tank to refill slowly over the day, the unit produces at that flow rate on demand, straight into whatever you're filling.

Translate that into an actual water change. A commonly cited hobbyist guideline for a mixed reef is a 10–15% weekly volume change — call it 4 to 6 gallons on a 40-gallon tank, or 12 to 18 gallons on a 120-gallon tank. At 50 GPD the smaller change is roughly two to three hours of continuous production; the larger one eats most of a day. At 75 GPD the 120-gallon change drops to an hour and a half to just over two hours. On the VonWater's quoted 0.42 GPM, that same 12 to 18 gallons fills in well under 45 minutes, because the water comes out at a fixed flow rate rather than trickling into a pressure tank between draws.

Planted-tank keepers often run deeper, more frequent changes — 20–50% weekly is common on a heavily fertilized, CO2-injected tank — so a 40% change on a 29-gallon tank is about 11.6 gallons, comfortably inside an hour on any of the three at their rated output. Daily auto top-off for evaporation on a reef is a smaller number, commonly 1 to 3 gallons a day on a small-to-medium tank, well inside even the slowest system's daily capacity. GPD mostly matters for the periodic big change, not the daily top-off.

The non-electric design is worth flagging on its own. The Express Water RO5DX runs on line pressure with no pump, which also means no pump noise and nothing to fail electrically — relevant if the system is going in a stand next to a display tank rather than a basement sump; we compare non-electric and tankless designs on exactly that tradeoff in quietest reverse osmosis systems. Its listing also includes a built-in leak detector as part of the box, worth knowing before you plumb anything in next to a sump stand you can't afford to flood — see our roundup of systems built around leak detection if that's the deciding feature for your setup. The VonWater needs a dedicated outlet and two drilled holes (one for the faucet, one for the drain line under the sink), and its 600 GPD number comes from a 7-stage tankless design — for how stage count scales, or doesn't, with output on other tankless systems, see 4 stages vs. 10 at 800 GPD.

DI resin: the stage serious reef keepers add after the membrane

A healthy RO membrane alone typically brings tap water with a TDS commonly in the hundreds down into roughly the high single digits to around 10–15 ppm — a large reduction, and good enough for most freshwater fish-only tanks and planted setups. It is not zero. For coral specifically, which can react to trace contaminants at concentrations a basic TDS meter barely registers, many reef keepers add a second stage after the membrane: a deionization (DI) cartridge, usually a mixed-bed ion-exchange resin, that polishes whatever the membrane let through down to an effectively unmeasurable reading. The combination has its own shorthand in the hobby — RO/DI.

None of the three systems here ship with a DI stage in the box, and that's normal at this price point: DI is almost universally sold as a standalone add-on canister rather than bundled into a $140–$220 under-sink system. Where you'd add one differs by design. The Express Water RO5DX and iSpring RCC7-BN are both classic multi-housing tank systems built on standard-size canisters, which makes plumbing a DI canister onto the output line after the membrane a straightforward addition. The VonWater is a sealed tankless unit with its own proprietary cartridge body; intercepting its output stream for an aftermarket DI stage is less obviously built in, so check VonWater's own installation manual and port layout before assuming you can add one — we don't have that detail from the listing, and we're not going to guess at it.

Resin exhausts fast when it's doing the membrane's job for it, so DI only makes economic sense downstream of an RO membrane that's already removing most of the load — running raw tap water through a DI canister alone burns through resin in days rather than months. If you're keeping hardy freshwater fish or a lightly stocked planted tank, most hobbyists skip DI entirely and run RO alone. It earns its cost mainly once coral is actually in the tank — our guide to systems with the longest change intervals and our breakdown of buying filters in bulk both cover the running-cost side of a cartridge schedule that, for an aquarium-heavy household, turns over faster than the 6–12 month intervals those parts are rated for on an average kitchen.

Reading output TDS — the number reef keepers chase to zero

A handheld TDS meter is the cheapest diagnostic tool in this hobby, and the number it reports means something different here than it does for a drinking-water buyer. A drinking-water RO system is usually judged to be working anywhere comfortably under 50 ppm. Reef keepers watch for something much closer to zero: 0–1 ppm after a membrane-plus-DI setup is the commonly cited target for anything going near coral, because small, un-dosed swings in chemistry matter more to a coral colony than to a tropical fish. RO alone, without a DI polishing stage, more typically lands in the high single digits to around 10–15 ppm — fine for a planted tank or a fish-only setup, a step short of what most reef keepers consider finished.

None of the three systems in this guide lists a built-in digital TDS readout in its published specification — no display on the faucet, no inline meter in the box. That's normal at this price range; digital TDS and filter-life displays tend to show up on pricier tankless flagships elsewhere in our under-sink lineup, not on a $143–$220 system. Budget a separate handheld meter, typically well under $20, and take your baseline reading the day you install the system: measure the feed tap water first, then the RO output, so later readings have a reference point.

Where you take the reading matters as much as whether you take it. A reading off the membrane alone tells you about the membrane, which fouls gradually — the number drifts up over weeks or months. A reading taken after a DI stage tells you about the resin instead, which fails on a completely different pattern: the bed holds at near-zero and then jumps all at once when it's spent, rather than drifting. Testing both points separately, not just the final tap, is how you know which stage actually needs changing instead of guessing from one number at the end of the line.

FAQ

Questions aquarium keepers ask about RO

Do I need reverse osmosis at all, or will dechlorinated tap water work?

For hardy freshwater fish-only tanks, treated tap water plus a standard dechlorinator is often enough. Reef tanks and heavily planted, CO2-injected tanks are a different case: corals and sensitive plants react to the phosphate, silicate, copper and trace-element baseline that a dechlorinator does nothing about, which is why RO or RO/DI is close to a given on the reef side of the hobby and less universal elsewhere. This is general information, not species-specific husbandry advice — check the requirements for your own livestock and plants.

Why not just buy one of the alkaline systems from your main roundup instead?

Because the alkaline stage on those systems adds calcium and magnesium back after the membrane at a ratio chosen for human taste, not matched to any aquarium salt mix or fertilizer schedule. For drinking water that's a genuine improvement; for a reef or planted tank it reintroduces an unmeasured mineral input under the measured one you're trying to control with salt mix or fertilizer. A plain system with no remineralization stage is the better starting point.

How long does making enough water for a weekly change actually take?

It depends on the system's rated GPD and the size of the change. A 12–18 gallon change — a common 10–15% weekly volume for a 120-gallon reef — runs roughly a day at 50 GPD, about 90 minutes to just over two hours at 75 GPD, and under 45 minutes on a tankless system producing at a quoted flow rate like the VonWater's 0.42 GPM. Smaller tanks and smaller changes scale down proportionally.

Is a DI resin stage required, or is the RO membrane enough on its own?

For most freshwater fish-only and planted tanks, RO alone — typically landing in the high single digits to around 10–15 ppm TDS — is enough. Reef keepers running coral commonly add a deionization (DI) cartridge after the membrane to polish that down toward 0–1 ppm, since coral can react to trace levels a plain membrane doesn't fully remove. DI is sold separately from every system in this guide and makes the most economic sense installed after a membrane that's already doing most of the work, not in place of one.
