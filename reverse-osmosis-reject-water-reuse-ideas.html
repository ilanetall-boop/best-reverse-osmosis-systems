What to Do With RO Reject Water Instead of Sending It Down the Drain

By Ilane TallUpdated October 11, 202613 min readResearch-based

Every reverse osmosis system under a kitchen sink runs on the same trade: for every gallon of clean, low-TDS water that comes out of the dedicated faucet, more goes down the drain line as what the industry calls reject water, concentrate, or brine. It's not a malfunction — it's how the membrane separates dissolved solids from the water passing through it, and every residential RO unit does it to some degree. The question most owners never ask, because nobody puts it on the box, is where that second stream goes once it leaves the drain saddle. The honest answer: straight into the sewer line, paid for twice — once when the utility delivered it, and again on the bill that measures what left your house.

This guide covers what's actually dissolved in reject water and why it isn't a contamination problem, which household jobs it suits and which plants will sulk if you use it carelessly, a simple way to divert it without cutting into your existing drain saddle, the short list of places it should never go, and roughly how many gallons a typical household has on hand to redirect in the first place.

What's actually in reject water — concentrated minerals, not contamination

A reverse osmosis membrane forces tap water through a semi-permeable barrier that lets water molecules pass while blocking larger dissolved minerals and salts — calcium, magnesium, sodium, sulfates, chlorides, whatever was in the feed water to begin with. The water that makes it through is the low-TDS permeate that fills your glass. The water that doesn't make it through carries everything that got rejected, and because that stream is smaller than the feed stream, the dissolved solids end up concentrated rather than diluted. Depending on the system's recovery rate, reject water commonly runs two to four times the total dissolved solids of your incoming tap water — not a fixed number, since it tracks how much of the feed stream becomes usable permeate.

That matters because "concentrated" and "contaminated" are not the same word. Reject water hasn't picked up anything new — it's the identical tap water you'd have gotten from the faucet without an RO system, with the minerals that were always in your local supply bunched up instead of spread through a larger volume. If your municipal water report lists hardness, sodium, or sulfate levels, reject water is simply those numbers multiplied — not a mystery liquid, not industrial runoff.

One detail works in your favor: on most multi-stage systems, the carbon and sediment filters sit ahead of the membrane, specifically to protect it from chlorine and particulate before the water ever splits into permeate and reject. That means the reject stream has usually already had chlorine or chloramine stripped out by the time it reaches the drain line — relevant a little further down, when we get to watering plants. What it has not had removed is the mineral load, which is the opposite side of what we cover in what an alkaline or remineralization stage actually changes.

Roughly how many gallons a household actually has to work with

This is the number most buying guides skip, and it's worth being precise about the assumptions rather than quoting one scary ratio, because the answer depends on which kind of system is under the sink. Classic tank systems historically ran as wasteful as 3:1 or 4:1 — three or four gallons rejected for every gallon produced. Newer tankless systems are commonly built closer to 1:1 or 2:1, and some non-electric designs now claim a ratio better than 1:1 in the owner's favor. None of that is fixed; it's a function of system design and incoming pressure, so check the specification for the unit you own rather than assuming a number.

A worked example, assumptions stated up front: a two-person household drinking and cooking with roughly 2 gallons of RO-filtered water a day, at a 3:1 drain ratio, produces about 6 gallons of reject water daily — roughly 180 gallons a month. At a more efficient 2:1 ratio, the same 2 gallons of permeate produces 4 gallons of reject, about 120 gallons a month. Scale that up for a larger household running more through the faucet — a kettle, a pet bowl, an ice maker — and the reject volume climbs with it; a family pulling 6 gallons of permeate a day at 3:1 is sending north of 500 gallons a month down the drain, more than most people picture.

Two things change that number. A permeate pump recovers drain pressure to push more water into the storage tank per cycle, lowering the effective ratio on systems built to use one. A self-cleaning or auto-flush system, on the other hand, periodically runs extra water across the membrane purely to clear scale, on top of the normal drain stream, raising the real monthly total. And households running a higher-output system — the kind covered in our guide to 1000+ GPD systems for large kitchens — are by definition producing a proportionally larger reject stream. Find your system's published ratio and do the gallon math for your own household rather than borrowing someone else's number.

Watering plants: which ones tolerate it, and which ones don't

Because reject water has already had chlorine and chloramine stripped out by the pre-filter stages, it's gentler on plants than straight tap water in one specific way. The trade-off is the concentrated mineral and salt load, and that's what decides which plants are a good match and which should stay on plain water.

Most ordinary houseplants and outdoor garden plants tolerate moderately hard, mineral-rich water without complaint — close to what falls as rain in many hard-water regions, minus the chlorine. Succulents and cacti are particularly tolerant, adapted to mineral-heavy, infrequent watering already. Established trees, shrubs, and lawn areas generally absorb occasional reject water without issue, especially as one input among several over a season.

The plants to be careful with are the acid-loving and salt-sensitive ones: azaleas, rhododendrons, blueberries, camellias, and most ferns and orchids prefer acidic, low-mineral conditions, and repeated watering with concentrated reject water can push soil pH and salinity the wrong way over months. African violets and other fussy houseplants fall into the same category. The telltale sign of trouble is browning or crisping at the leaf tips — a classic symptom of salt buildup in the root zone, and the cue to flush the soil with plain water.

A few habits keep this simple. Dilute reject water roughly 50/50 with plain tap or rainwater for anything you're unsure about, rather than using it straight. Rotate it with plain watering so minerals don't accumulate in the soil unflushed. And skip it for seedlings and young transplants, whose roots are more sensitive to salinity than mature plants. None of this is a reason to avoid reusing reject water in the garden — it's a reason to treat it like any moderately hard water source, which is exactly what it is.

Flushing toilets, mopping floors, and other low-stakes household uses

The easiest category of reuse is anything where mineral content doesn't matter, because the water isn't being consumed or touching anything sensitive to hardness. Flushing toilets is the obvious one — pour a bucket into the bowl before flushing, or lift the tank lid and pour it in directly. Mopping floors is another: reject water cleans tile, vinyl, and sealed hardwood the same way tap water does, and since the chlorine is already stripped out, it won't leave the faint bleachy smell tap water sometimes does on a warm floor.

Outdoor cleaning jobs fit too — rinsing a car's exterior, hosing down a patio or driveway, pre-rinsing muddy boots or garden tools. Pre-rinsing dishes before the dishwasher works as well, since the dishwasher's own detergent and hot-water cycle handle sanitizing regardless. Topping off a mop bucket, an outdoor pet bowl, or a birdbath are the same low-stakes logic: nothing about being concentrated tap water makes it unsuitable for the job.

The one place to think twice is anything involving a steam appliance — a steam iron, a steam mop, or a humidifier. Those are built around low-mineral water specifically because minerals precipitate out as scale inside the heating element, and reject water is the opposite of what they want. Stick to your RO system's actual permeate for anything that heats water to the point of leaving mineral deposits behind.

Rigging a simple diverter without re-plumbing the drain saddle

The drain saddle — the clamp fitting that taps into your sink's drain pipe to accept the RO system's reject line — is the part most people assume they'd have to disturb to capture this water, and it's the part you don't need to touch. The reject line running from the RO unit to the saddle is almost always standard 1/4-inch push-to-connect tubing, and that tubing is exactly where a simple diverter belongs.

The job in four steps:

  1. Find the reject line. Trace the tubing from the back of the RO unit to where it connects to the drain saddle on the sink's drain pipe. It's usually the thinnest of the two or three tubes running to the unit, separate from the feed-water line and the line to the faucet.
  2. Cut in a diverter valve. A push-to-connect tee fitting or a dedicated inline diverter valve — sold at any hardware store or plumbing supply counter for the same 1/4-inch tubing size — can be inserted directly into that existing run with a tubing cutter, no tools beyond that and no need to unclamp the saddle itself.
  3. Route a second line to a container. Run a short length of matching tubing from the new tee or valve down into a bucket, a large jug, or a dedicated catch container kept under the sink. A valve lets you choose, on any given day, whether the reject stream goes to the drain as usual or into your container.
  4. Empty it on a schedule that fits your use. A 5-gallon bucket fills faster than most people expect at a 3:1 ratio — refer back to the gallon math above for your own system — so plan to empty it into the garden, the mop bucket, or the toilet tank before it overflows, rather than leaving it to collect indefinitely.

Two cautions before you start. Never fully block the original drain path with a valve left in the wrong position — the point is a diverter, not a dead end, and a reject line with nowhere to go can back up against the membrane. And if your reject line runs to an air gap fitting built into the faucet rather than directly to a saddle, confirm the tubing layout before cutting anything, since the air gap prevents backflow and shouldn't be bypassed. If in doubt, ask whoever installed the system, or the manufacturer's support line, rather than guessing.

What not to do: aquariums, drinking, and reptile tanks

The same concentration that makes reject water harmless for mopping a floor makes it a poor choice anywhere water chemistry has to sit inside a narrow range. Aquariums are the clearest example: fish and aquatic invertebrates are adapted to a specific range of hardness, TDS, and mineral balance for their species, and dumping in a mineral-concentrated, unpredictable stream — one whose exact TDS varies with your local water report and your system's recovery rate — risks pushing the tank outside that range. If you keep fish, use your RO system's actual permeate, blended to the hardness your species needs. This is general information about water chemistry, not a substitute for species-specific aquarium care guidance.

Reptile tanks carry a related but distinct risk. Enclosures often rely on misting or standing water for humidity, and concentrated mineral water left to evaporate in a warm terrarium leaves behind visible white mineral scale on glass, decor, and heating elements faster than ordinary tap water would — a maintenance headache more than a safety one, but a real reason to skip it. As with aquariums, this is general information, not veterinary advice.

Drinking reject water yourself is the last item, and it's less a safety line than a pointless-defeats-the-purpose line. It isn't treated differently for pathogens than your regular tap water — the carbon and sediment stages ahead of the membrane already did that work for both streams. The actual problem is that it's the concentrated version of your tap water's mineral and sodium content, the opposite of why most people install an RO system. Use the faucet the system was built to produce drinking water from, and put the reject stream to work on the jobs in this guide instead.

FAQ

Questions people ask about reusing reject water

Is RO reject water safe to use elsewhere in the house?

For non-drinking uses, yes. Reject water is the same tap water your utility delivered, just with dissolved minerals concentrated rather than contaminated, and on most systems it has already had chlorine stripped out by the pre-filter stages ahead of the membrane. It's a good fit for flushing toilets, mopping floors, outdoor cleaning, and watering tolerant plants. It's a poor fit for aquariums, reptile enclosures, and drinking, where concentrated minerals matter.

Can I water all my plants with reject water?

Most ordinary houseplants, succulents, and established garden plants tolerate it fine, especially diluted roughly 50/50 with plain water and rotated with normal watering. Acid-loving and salt-sensitive plants — azaleas, blueberries, ferns, orchids, African violets — are the exceptions, since concentrated minerals can push soil pH and salinity against what they need. Watch for browning leaf tips, the classic sign of salt buildup, and switch to plain water if you see it.

Do I need a plumber to add a diverter to my reject line?

Usually not. The reject line is standard 1/4-inch push-to-connect tubing running from the RO unit to the drain saddle, and a push-to-connect tee or inline diverter valve — available at any hardware store — can be cut into that existing tubing with a basic tubing cutter, without disturbing the saddle clamp itself. It's a project most people handle in under fifteen minutes.

How much reject water does a typical household actually produce?

It depends on your system's drain ratio and how much permeate you use. As a worked example: two people using about 2 gallons of RO water a day at a 3:1 drain ratio produce roughly 6 gallons of reject water daily, or about 180 gallons a month; at a more efficient 2:1 ratio, the same usage produces about 120 gallons a month. Larger households and higher-output systems scale that number up proportionally — check your own system's published ratio rather than assuming one.
