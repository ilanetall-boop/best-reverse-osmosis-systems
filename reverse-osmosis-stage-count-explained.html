RO Stage Count Explained: Why 11 Stages Isn't Twice as Good as 5

By Ilane TallUpdated October 16, 202615 min readResearch-based

Scroll through under-sink reverse osmosis listings on Amazon and the headline number does a lot of the selling: 5-stage, 7-stage, 10-stage, 11-stage. The instinct is to treat it like a spec you can rank on, the way you'd rank megapixels or horsepower. Higher must be better, and an 11-stage system must be doing more than twice the work of a 5-stage one.

It isn't, and the reason is that "stage" is not a defined unit. No certifying body hands out a stamp for stage count the way NSF/ANSI 58 certifies that a system actually reduces total dissolved solids. A stage is whatever the manufacturer decides to call a stage — a housing, a cartridge, a mineral ball, a coating on a cartridge, a lamp. Two companies building functionally similar systems can land on different numbers simply because one of them split a single carbon cartridge's job into two line items on the box and the other didn't.

That is exactly what you can see across four real listings. The iSpring RCC7-BN is sold as a 5-stage system. The iSpring RCC7AK-UVBN is 7. The Express Water ROALK5D is 10. The Express Water ROALKUV10D is 11. None of those jumps happen because the water gets filtered more thoroughly in some fundamental sense. They happen because of what got added on top of — and how finely the box split up — the same small set of jobs every one of these systems has to do.

The three stages no RO system can skip

Strip the marketing off any of these four boxes and there are exactly three jobs that have to happen, in this order, or what comes out of the faucet isn't reverse osmosis water at all.

Sediment. A spun or pleated filter catching sand, rust flakes and particulate before anything else touches the water. It is the cheapest, crudest stage in the system, and it exists as much to protect everything downstream as to clean the water itself.

Carbon. Activated carbon stripping chlorine, chloramine and the taste and odor that come with them. This stage is not a courtesy — free chlorine will attack and shorten the life of the thin-film composite membrane that comes next, so carbon is doing protective work as much as cosmetic work.

The membrane. The actual reverse-osmosis step: a semi-permeable membrane that rejects dissolved solids down to a fraction of a micron, under pressure, sending part of the feed water to drain as reject. Take this stage away and there is no RO system left, just a carbon filter.

Sediment, carbon, membrane — that's the floor. Almost every listing adds one more thing on top as a near-default fourth: a post-carbon polish after the membrane, giving the water a final pass before it reaches the faucet. The iSpring RCC7-BN's own "5-stage" count is a clean illustration of how the arithmetic works: sediment is stage one, the carbon job gets split into a granular-carbon stage and a carbon-block stage (two and three), the membrane is stage four, and the post-carbon polish is stage five. Three hard requirements, one near-universal addition, and the number on the box already depends on whether the carbon job got counted once or twice.

Where the bigger numbers actually come from

Once you can see that a 5-stage listing is really "three required jobs plus one split plus one near-default extra," the jump to 7, 10 and 11 stops looking mysterious. It comes from three places, and a given system can use any combination of them.

1. Splitting sediment and carbon further

Nothing stops a manufacturer from running two sediment stages at different micron ratings instead of one, or breaking the carbon job into granular carbon, then a carbon block, then a second carbon block. Each split is a real, physically distinct cartridge — it isn't fake — but it's also not a new capability. It's the same pre-filtration job, counted in smaller pieces. A listing that reaches 10 stages typically gets several of them this way before a single drop of water has reached the membrane.

2. Adding an alkaline or remineralization stage

The membrane strips out dissolved minerals along with everything else it rejects, and an alkaline cartridge puts some back — typically a blend of mineral media like calcium and magnesium, sometimes alongside tourmaline or infrared balls marketed for pH. Manufacturers building an "alkaline" system commonly count each mineral media type inside that cartridge as its own stage rather than one combined stage, which is how a single remineralization add-on can turn into three or four extra numbers on the box almost at once. Whether any of that remineralization changes anything that matters for your health is a separate question from the stage count, and it's one we work through on its own in does alkaline RO water actually do anything.

3. Adding a UV lamp

A UV sterilization stage sits after the storage tank and irradiates the water on its way to the faucet, targeting bacteria that could otherwise multiply in stagnant tank water. It is the one add-on in this list that needs electricity to do anything. Look at our four systems again and the UV stage is the entire difference between two otherwise comparable pairs: it's the gap between the iSpring RCC7-BN (5, no UV) and the RCC7AK-UVBN (7, alkaline and UV added), and it's the exact one-stage gap between the Express Water ROALK5D (10, alkaline, no UV) and the ROALKUV10D (11, alkaline plus UV).

Everything that separates 5 from 11 happens on either side of that single row — more pre-filter splitting, an alkaline add-on, a UV lamp — and none of it makes the membrane itself reject more dissolved solids than it otherwise would.

Does more stages mean more maintenance — and what does that cost?

Partly yes, but not in the way the headline number implies. More stages generally does mean more individual cartridges to track and swap, because each one is a physical part with its own service life. A 5-stage system has three consumable stages ahead of the long-lived membrane — sediment, granular carbon, carbon block — plus the post-carbon polish after it. An 11-stage alkaline-and-UV system adds an alkaline cartridge (or several, bundled as one physical part even when counted as multiple stages) and a UV lamp on top of that same base. That's more individual change events over a year, even though the jump from 5 to 11 looks like it more than doubled.

What it is not is a proportional increase in your filter bill. The sediment and carbon stages are the cheapest parts in any of these systems regardless of how many of them there are, and the single most expensive consumable — the membrane — doesn't change count or price based on the stage number on the box; both a 5-stage and an 11-stage system have exactly one. An alkaline cartridge adds a real, ongoing cost on its own schedule, and a UV lamp is a periodic replacement with its own cost on top of that — both genuine additions to your annual bill, but additions tied to the specific features you bought, not to "having more stages" as a category. If a 10- or 11-stage listing's price difference over a simpler system is bundling a spare filter set at checkout, that changes the first-year math — we break down what that's actually worth in RO systems that ship with a spare filter set.

Stage count also tells you nothing about how fast a system fills or how it handles weak incoming pressure — that's a function of the GPD rating and, for homes with low pressure, whether the unit includes a booster pump. The number that predicts filtration quality, and the one that's mostly marketing

If you can only look at one number before buying, make it the certification claim, not the stage count. NSF/ANSI 58 is a performance certification — it tests and certifies that a system actually reduces total dissolved solids and specific contaminants by a verified amount. A stage count is not tested or certified by anyone; it is simply how the manufacturer chose to describe its own box.

Our four listings make the contrast concrete without any guesswork: both iSpring systems — the 5-stage RCC7-BN and the 7-stage RCC7AK-UVBN — state "NSF Certified" directly in their listing titles. Neither Express Water listing in this comparison makes that claim in its title, despite one of them carrying the highest stage count of the four. Stage count went up; the certification claim that actually says something about filtration performance did not appear at all. That's the pattern worth remembering every time a bigger number on a box is doing the persuading: ask what's certified, not just what's counted.

None of this means a 10- or 11-stage alkaline-and-UV system is a bad buy — alkaline remineralization and UV sterilization are real features that real buyers want for real reasons, and if you want them, buying a system that includes them beats bolting them on separately. It means you should want them because you looked them up individually, not because "11" felt like more filtration than "5."

How to read a stage breakdown before you assume higher is better

  • Find the certification line first. NSF/ANSI 58, WQA, or a named equivalent tells you something tested. A stage count printed in bold on the box tells you how the manufacturer chose to count, and nothing else.
  • Count how many stages come before the membrane. If most of the headline number is pre-filter splitting — a second sediment stage, a third carbon stage — you're mostly looking at more thorough protection for the membrane, not a system that rejects more contaminants once water reaches it.
  • Separate "stage" from "feature." Alkaline and UV are genuine features with their own costs and their own value propositions. Evaluate each on its own merits — do you want remineralized water, do you want UV sterilization after the tank — rather than letting them inflate a single number you then compare like a spec.
  • Check the GPD rating and drain ratio separately. Neither is implied by the stage count, and both affect how the system actually performs at your sink day to day.
  • If you're shopping secondhand or a Renewed listing, verify the stage breakdown against the physical housings, not just the title. A reseller can carry over a stage count from the original listing without it being obvious whether every cartridge type is still present. Our guides on buying a used reverse osmosis system and whether an Amazon Renewed discount is worth the risk both cover what to confirm before you buy.

The short version: stage count is a useful shorthand for "what features did the manufacturer bundle in," and a poor shorthand for "how well does this filter my water." Treat it the first way and the number on the box stops being confusing.

FAQ

Questions people ask about stage counts

Does more stages mean better filtration?

Not reliably. The stage that actually removes dissolved solids is the membrane, and every system in this comparison — 5-stage or 11-stage — has exactly one. Extra stages are usually finer pre-filter splitting, an alkaline remineralization add-on, or a UV lamp, not a deeper filtration path. A certification like NSF/ANSI 58 tells you more about verified performance than the stage count does.

How many stages does a reverse osmosis system actually need?

Three, structurally: a sediment stage, a carbon stage, and the membrane itself. Almost every system adds a fourth, a post-carbon polish, as a near-default. Anything past that — a split carbon job, alkaline remineralization, UV sterilization — is an added feature, not a filtration requirement.

Do more stages mean more filters to replace?

Generally yes — more physical cartridges means more individual change events over a year. But the cost impact is tied to which features were added, not to the stage number itself: the membrane, the single most expensive consumable, costs and counts the same whether the system is rated 5 stages or 11. Alkaline cartridges and UV lamps add real, specific costs on their own schedules.

Is an 11-stage system worth paying more for than a 5-stage one?

It can be, if you specifically want what the extra stages add. Comparing our four listings, the Express Water ROALKUV10D (11 stages, $249.99) adds alkaline remineralization and UV sterilization over the iSpring RCC7-BN (5 stages, $219.96) — real features with real value to some households. It's a poor reason to pay more if the only thing being compared is the number itself, since neither system's single membrane does more work than the other's.
