Reverse osmosis water for plants and seedlings: when stripped-down water helps and when it doesn't

By Ilane TallUpdated November 28, 202613 min readResearch-based

Search "reverse osmosis for plants" and you land in the same conversation we already had in our guide to RO for aquariums and reef tanks: a hobbyist reaching for the exact same hardware a drinking-water buyer uses, but wanting the opposite thing from it. A reef keeper doesn't want RO water because it's pure for its own sake — they want it because it's a known, repeatable starting point they can build a salt mix on top of. A grower chasing RO water for seedlings and sensitive container plants is running the same logic in a different hobby.

Municipal tap water carries a handful of additives that are entirely reasonable from a public-health standpoint and mildly hostile from a potting-soil standpoint. Chlorine or chloramine, added as a disinfectant, is the one most growers have heard of. Sodium, often elevated on water that's passed through a residential softener, is one most haven't — softened water trades calcium and magnesium for sodium, and sodium builds up in a pot the way it never does in open ground, because there's no deep soil column to leach it away. Fluoride, added in many municipal systems for dental health, is a non-issue for almost every plant and a real one for a short list of specific species. None of these show up as a taste problem, because nobody is drinking the water; they show up weeks later as leaf-tip burn, stunted roots, or a seedling tray that simply never took off, with a cause that's easy to miss unless you already suspect the water.

Reverse osmosis strips essentially all of that out, dissolved solids included, and hands the grower a blank slate instead — water with close to nothing in it, so that whatever goes into the watering can afterward is the only variable left. That's the actual case for RO in a growing setup: not purity as a virtue, but control. Whether your plants need that control is a different question, and the honest answer is that most of them don't.

Which plants actually react to tap water — and which don't notice

The plants with a real, well-documented sensitivity to tap water are a specific, fairly short list, not "all houseplants." Carnivorous plants — Venus flytraps, sundews, pitcher plants — are the clearest case: they evolved in nutrient-poor bogs with naturally soft, mineral-free water, and the dissolved salts and chlorine in most municipal supplies are commonly cited as a leading cause of a slow decline in these species specifically, which is why rainwater, distilled water or RO water is the standard recommendation for them rather than a nice-to-have. Delicate seedlings generally sit in the same category for a different reason: a young taproot has none of the buffering capacity a mature root system has, so the same chlorine level a six-month-old plant shrugs off can set back a two-week-old one.

A second group is sensitive less to chlorine and more to accumulated salts and alkalinity over repeated waterings. Acid-loving plants — blueberries, azaleas, many ferns — want soil in a specific lower pH range, and tap water that runs alkaline (common where municipal supplies are treated with lime, or anywhere on hard groundwater) slowly pushes container soil pH upward with every watering, in a way open-ground plantings rarely experience because rain and drainage keep diluting and flushing it. African violets and some orchids are commonly flagged for the same accumulated-salt sensitivity, plus a specific intolerance for fluoride that shows up as brown leaf-tip scorch over months of tap watering. What the membrane removes versus what carbon removes matters here too — chlorine and chloramine are stopped mainly by the carbon stages ahead of the membrane, not the membrane itself, which is why a system's carbon-stage quality matters as much as its GPD rating for this specific use.

Everything else on the list is more tolerant than growers often assume. Succulents, most vegetables, most common houseplants and the bulk of what's in a typical outdoor bed handle tap water fine, especially if it's left in an open container for a day so chlorine (though not chloramine, which doesn't off-gas the same way) has a chance to dissipate before use. If nothing you grow is on the sensitive list above, RO water is solving a problem you don't have — which is also the honest answer to a question we'll come back to directly in the FAQ below.

Why an alkaline stage works against you here — the same logic as a reef tank, for a different reason

Everywhere else on this site, when we're talking about drinking water, an alkaline or remineralization stage is a point in a system's favor: stripped-down RO water can taste flat, so systems like the one in our main under-sink roundup add a mineral cartridge after the membrane specifically to put calcium and magnesium back in, calibrated for human taste. For a grower dosing nutrients, that same stage is a defect for exactly the reason it's a defect in a reef tank: it reintroduces an unmeasured mineral load, at a ratio chosen for how water tastes in a glass, underneath the measured nutrient load you're trying to control.

Of the three systems in this guide, the APEC Water ROES-PH75 is the one built around that alkaline stage — a six-stage system with the mineral cartridge as a core feature, which is exactly right for someone filling a drinking glass and exactly wrong for someone filling a nutrient reservoir. If it's the system you already own, the fix usually isn't replacing it: on most alkaline RO systems the remineralization cartridge sits in its own dedicated housing, separate from the sediment, carbon and membrane stages, and bypassing that one housing returns the output to plain RO. Check the specific listing or manual before assuming that's true of your unit — we don't have that detail confirmed for every alkaline system on the market, so we're not going to assert it as universal.

This is the mirror image of a separate case we cover in our guide to RO water for coffee and espresso, where near-zero TDS is also the wrong target — but for a taste reason, not a dosing one. Growers and espresso brewers both end up avoiding the alkaline add-on, for two completely different reasons that happen to produce the same recommendation. The two plain systems here, the iSpring RCC7-BN and the APEC RO-90, skip the mineral stage entirely and hand you a true near-zero baseline, which is the starting point an actual nutrient program is built to assume.

Dosing from zero versus dosing against a number you don't know

Every commercial liquid nutrient line — the feeding schedules printed on the bottle, the EC or PPM targets a feeding chart tells you to hit — is formulated assuming the water it's mixed into starts at or close to zero dissolved solids. That assumption is explicit in how the charts are written: "mix to 1.2 mS/cm EC" only means what it says if your starting water is near 0 mS/cm. Tap water never is. Depending on the municipal supply, it might carry an EC baseline anywhere from under 0.1 mS/cm on soft surface water to well over 0.5 mS/cm on hard groundwater, and that baseline is not printed on your water bill and changes with the seasons and with utility maintenance work you'll never hear about.

Mix a full-strength feed into tap water carrying its own unknown EC and the number on your meter afterward is the sum of two things: the nutrient dose you intentionally added, and a tap-water baseline you didn't measure and can't isolate. At low concentrations the difference is cosmetic. At the concentrations seedlings and young transplants are fed — deliberately diluted well below adult strength, because young roots have almost no tolerance for excess salts — that unmeasured baseline can be the difference between a safe dilution and a dose that causes nutrient burn, and you'd have no way to know which, because you never had a true starting number.

RO water removes that uncertainty by giving you an actual zero (or close to it, typically under 10 ppm from the membrane alone) to dose against. A cheap handheld EC or TDS meter, used the same way as in a reef setup, turns feeding from a recipe you hope is right into a number you can verify before it ever reaches a root. That matters most for anyone running a calibrated feeding program on seedlings, microgreens or a hydroponic or semi-hydroponic setup — it matters far less if you're watering an established raised bed with a slow-release granular feed, where the soil itself buffers out small variations tap water would introduce.

How much RO water a watering cycle actually needs

GPD — gallons per day — is the output rating printed on every RO system's box, and like most specs in this category it's measured under favorable lab conditions: higher incoming pressure and warmer feed water than a lot of real houses deliver. Treat the printed number as a ceiling, not a guarantee, and the gap between container gardening and anything greenhouse-scale is large enough that the same system can be comfortably oversized for one job and undersized for the other.

Container and seedling-tray watering is a small-volume job. A 4-inch seedling cell takes a few ounces; a 6-inch nursery pot takes roughly a quart to half-gallon per watering; a windowsill or balcony setup of twenty mixed containers, watered a couple of times a week, lands somewhere around 8 to 15 gallons a week in total. At the 75 GPD rating on the iSpring RCC7-BN, that entire week's water comes off the tap in under three hours of total run time, typically filled into jugs between waterings rather than drawn live. For someone in this range who isn't ready to commit to permanent under-sink plumbing — a renter, or a balcony grower without a kitchen to modify — a countertop unit under $200 covers the same small volume without a drilled faucet hole or a drain saddle.

A small greenhouse or a dedicated hydroponic reservoir is a different order of magnitude. A single 20-gallon nutrient reservoir refreshed weekly, plus daily top-offs of a gallon or two for evaporation and plant uptake, can put a greenhouse grower into the 25 to 40 gallon-a-week range even at a modest scale — and that's before counting a full reservoir dump-and-refill, which some growers do every one to two weeks rather than topping off indefinitely. At 75 GPD, filling a 20-gallon reservoir from empty takes a little under six and a half hours; at the 90 GPD the APEC RO-90 is rated for, the same reservoir fills in a bit over five hours. Neither system is "fast" in the tankless sense — nothing in this guide has a storage tank or a pump — but for a reservoir filled overnight or over an afternoon rather than on demand, the difference between 75 and 90 GPD is the difference that actually matters at this scale, more than any spec sheet number that sounds impressive and isn't.

One number worth sitting with before you commit to running everything through RO: every gallon an RO membrane produces sends a second gallon or more down the drain as reject water, at ratios that run anywhere from roughly 2:1 to 4:1 depending on the system. A grower producing 30 gallons a week of product water on a 3:1 system is also sending close to 90 gallons down the drain in the process. That's a real cost on a metered water bill and worth weighing against how many of your plants are actually on the sensitive list in the section above.

FAQ

Questions growers ask about RO water

Do all my houseplants need reverse osmosis water, or is tap fine?

For most houseplants, vegetables and succulents, plain tap water — left out uncovered for a day so chlorine can dissipate — is genuinely fine, and running it through an RO system just sends most of it down the drain as reject water for no benefit. RO is worth the switch for a specific, shorter list: carnivorous plants, acid-loving plants sensitive to alkalinity buildup like blueberries and some ferns, species known to react to fluoride such as some orchids and African violets, and seedlings or young transplants whose roots have little tolerance for chlorine or accumulated salts. This is general growing information, not species-specific horticultural advice — check what your particular plants actually need.

Why is an alkaline RO system the wrong choice for feeding nutrients?

Because the alkaline stage adds calcium and magnesium back into the water at a ratio chosen for human taste, not matched to any fertilizer program. A nutrient feeding chart assumes the water it's mixed into starts near zero dissolved solids; an unmeasured mineral load sitting underneath your measured nutrient dose defeats that assumption, the same way it defeats a reef keeper's calibrated salt mix. A plain system with no remineralization stage, or an alkaline system with that stage bypassed, is the better starting point for dosing.

How much RO water does a seedling tray or a small greenhouse actually need?

Container and seedling-tray watering is a small-volume job — commonly 8 to 15 gallons a week for a sizeable windowsill or balcony collection, which a 75 GPD system produces in under three hours of total run time. A small greenhouse reservoir is a different scale: a 20-gallon nutrient reservoir with daily top-offs can run 25 to 40 gallons a week, and the gap between a 75 GPD and a 90 GPD system becomes the difference that matters for how long a refill actually takes.

Does reverse osmosis remove the chlorine that bothers sensitive plants?

Yes — chlorine and chloramine are stopped mainly by the carbon stages ahead of the RO membrane, not by the membrane itself, and a plain 5-stage system removes both along with the dissolved mineral salts the membrane handles. That combination is why RO water reads as both chlorine-free and near-zero TDS, which matters for carnivorous plants and sensitive seedlings in a way it doesn't for a plant that only cares about one of those two things.
