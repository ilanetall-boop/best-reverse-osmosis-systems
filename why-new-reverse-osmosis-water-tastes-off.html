New RO System Tastes Like Plastic or Chlorine? What's Normal and What Isn't

By Ilane TallUpdated September 22, 202615 min readResearch-based

We're reader-supported: buying through our links may earn us a commission at no extra cost to you. We compare published specifications, certifications and prices — we don't run a testing lab.

The first glass out of a brand-new reverse osmosis system is rarely the one on the box. Instead of tasting like nothing — the whole promise of the category — it can taste like the inside of a vinyl beach ball, or carry a faint plastic sweetness. That reaction is close to universal, and on its own it does not mean the system is faulty. What matters is how long it lasts, and whether a second, different taste joins it and never goes away.

This page works through the taste complaints that actually generate calls to manufacturer support lines: the new-system taste, a chlorine note that will not quit, a metallic or mineral edge, and the stale taste that shows up after the tap sits unused. Each traces back to a specific stage in the system, which is why "it just tastes bad" is the wrong way to describe the problem to anyone trying to fix it — including yourself.

Where the plastic-and-box smell actually comes from

A reverse osmosis system arrives as a stack of new parts: injection-molded housings, silicone and EPDM o-rings, polyethylene tubing, a food-grade storage tank with a rubber bladder inside it, and — on tankless units — a sealed plastic manifold. None of that is dangerous, and none of it is unusual for a product that sits boxed for months before installation. But it is not inert either: plasticizers and residual manufacturing compounds sit on those surfaces, and the first water through picks some of it up, the same way a new water bottle or garden hose tastes faintly of plastic before it has been rinsed a few times.

The storage tank is usually the biggest contributor, because it holds standing water against a large area of new rubber for hours before the first glass. A tankless system skips that reservoir, which is one reason its owners sometimes report a milder version of the same taste. Either way, the fix is displacement, not chemistry — you are rinsing the residue away, not neutralizing it.

Manufacturers are fairly consistent on the number: fill the storage tank, drain it fully through the faucet, and repeat two to three times before drinking the water. That clears the worst of it within the first few gallons on a tank system. Trace off-gassing from the tank liner and tubing can continue at a lower level for one to two weeks, so a faint edge that fades daily is expected; one that is just as strong on day ten as day one is not. If you skipped the initial flush when you installed the faucet, our guide to sanitizing and maintaining a system covers the same flush-and-drain sequence you should repeat at every future filter change, not just the first one. And if you are wondering why a "brand new" tank seems to empty faster than its rated capacity suggests, that is a separate, normal quirk of how the bladder holds pressure — covered in our storage tank guide — and unrelated to taste.

A chlorine taste that doesn't fade points at the carbon stage, not the membrane

Chlorine and chloramine are added by municipal utilities because they are effective and stable enough to survive the trip through miles of pipe to your kitchen. That stability is also why they are the hardest taste complaint for an RO system to fully erase, and why the job is handled by a dedicated stage rather than the membrane. Reverse osmosis systems put one or two carbon stages ahead of the membrane — typically a granular activated carbon (GAC) cartridge followed by a carbon block — and it is those cartridges, not the membrane, that adsorb chlorine and the taste and odor compounds riding with it.

Carbon matters for two reasons, and only one is about taste. Free chlorine is corrosive to the thin-film composite membrane used in nearly every residential RO system, so the carbon stages exist first to protect that expensive component, and only second to improve taste. A chlorine taste reaching your glass therefore means carbon that should have caught it upstream did not — and the membrane behind it has been exposed for however long that has been true.

The usual reason is straightforward exhaustion. Unlike a sediment filter, activated carbon does not visibly clog — it keeps passing water at full flow until its adsorption capacity runs out, with no other warning sign. Standard 10-inch housings are cheap and quick to replace with a wrench; a proprietary quick-change cartridge is faster to swap but only sold by one manufacturer — we cost out that trade-off in our quick-change versus universal filters comparison. A cartridge rated for six to twelve months on an assumed household can also run out sooner if your utility raises chlorine dosing after a main break or during warmer months — a common, unannounced reason water that tasted fine in spring starts tasting like a pool by midsummer.

For a standard 5-stage layout, the iSpring F7-GAC replacement pack is a 7-count, NSF-certified annual set covering sediment and carbon together, which keeps both stages on the same schedule. DREO systems are proprietary instead — the DREO 2-Pack replacement filter is built for the 112, 113, 114 and 115 models and rated for 265 gallons, the number to weigh against your own draw before assuming a lingering chlorine taste means the system is at fault rather than the filter simply being due.

A metallic or mineral edge is often the remineralization stage working as designed

Plain reverse osmosis water is close to mineral-free, and a lot of people find that flatness unpleasant — part of why "alkaline" and "remineralization" systems exist. Those systems add a final cartridge after the membrane that reintroduces calcium, magnesium, potassium and sometimes sodium on purpose, to round out the taste and nudge pH upward. If your system has that stage and the water suddenly tastes metallic or mineral-forward, the likelier explanation is not contamination — the cartridge is doing exactly the job it was built for, and you are noticing it for the first time.

The taste is usually most noticeable right after a filter change: a fresh mineral cartridge releases its media a little more readily in the first few gallons than once it settles into steady use, so a note that mellows over the first week is typical. It can also read stronger if your feed water already runs high in dissolved minerals, since the cartridge adds to whatever baseline survives the membrane's rejection.

The taste actually worth worrying about is different: a sharp metallic bite with no remineralization stage to explain it can point at corroding plumbing upstream of the system rather than anything the RO unit is adding — a plumbing question, worth raising with whoever installed your fixtures.

Stale or flat-tasting water after a few unused days has a specific fix

Come back from a long weekend, draw the first glass from an RO faucet that has not been touched in days, and it can taste noticeably duller or slightly plasticky — even on a system that has run fine for months. The cause is standing water: a tank holds several gallons against rubber bladder and plastic liner, and the longer it sits without turnover, the more trace taste it picks up while losing the dissolved gases that make fresh water taste crisp. Tankless systems are not immune either, just at a much smaller volume, in the manifold and the tubing run to the faucet.

The fix does not require a service call. Run the faucet for ten to fifteen seconds and let that stagnant volume clear before filling a glass — the same habit plumbers recommend for any fixture left unused for a while. Leaving for more than a week or two, draining the storage tank first and letting the system refill fresh on your return avoids the problem rather than just flushing it away after the fact.

One version is worth distinguishing: if stagnant water tastes actively off rather than just flat, or gets worse after a few flushes, that has crossed from expected staleness into needing a sanitizing pass. Bacteria and biofilm can establish in a tank or tubing that sits unused for extended stretches, especially months past the last cartridge change. Our sanitizing and maintenance guide covers the full routine, including the step most owners skip at filter-change time.

Read the certification for taste and odor, not just contaminant removal

Most buying guides — this site included — talk about certification almost entirely in terms of contaminant reduction: NSF/ANSI 58 for total dissolved solids and specific contaminants, NSF/ANSI 372 for lead-free materials. Neither is actually about taste. The one that is, and the one worth checking if taste and odor are your complaint, is NSF/ANSI 42 — the standard covering aesthetic effects, including chlorine taste and odor reduction, rather than health-related removal. A system can be excellent under 58 and still make no claim under 42, because the two are testing different things.

This is easy to miss because manufacturers often list several certifications on one line. The Frizzlife PD600-TAM3 mentioned above is listed against NSF/ANSI 58, 53, 42 and 372 together — 58 for contaminant reduction, 53 for health-effect claims, 42 for the taste-and-odor claim, 372 for lead-free materials. Four certifications, four jobs, and only one says anything about how the water is expected to taste. If a taste complaint sent you looking, 42 is the line to find — if it is not listed, no formal taste-and-odor claim has been made, whatever the marketing copy implies.

When a bad taste stops being a break-in period

Put the timelines from this page together and a rough rule falls out: the new-system taste should be fading noticeably within the first few gallons and largely gone within one to two weeks of normal use. A remineralization taste should settle within the first week after each cartridge change. A stagnant-tank taste should clear within a glass or two of flushing. Anything that fits one of those windows is a system behaving as designed, not a problem to solve.

What crosses into a real problem is a taste that holds steady — or worsens — well past its expected window, especially alongside a second symptom. A chlorine taste paired with a drop in flow usually means a clogged pre-filter is choking the whole system, not just letting chlorine through. A metallic taste with no remineralization stage installed is a plumbing question. A stale taste that intensifies instead of clearing after a flush is a sanitizing problem. In each case the fix is specific to the stage responsible rather than a system-wide replacement, which is why guessing at "the filter" in general costs more than diagnosing which cartridge is actually due. Our guide to how long an RO system and its parts actually last is a useful reference for whether a component's drop-off should be expected.

If none of that matches — the taste came out of nowhere on a system that has been fine for a long stretch, tastes genuinely foul rather than off, or is accompanied by cloudiness or a change in color — treat it as a signal to stop drinking the water and check the system physically rather than waiting it out. That is the one scenario on this page that is not a break-in period under any reading of it.

FAQ

Questions people ask about new RO system taste

How many flushes does it take to clear the plastic taste from a new RO system?

Two to three full fill-and-drain cycles through the storage tank clears most of it, which manufacturers generally recommend before drinking the water at all. A fainter trace can keep fading for one to two weeks of normal use as the tank liner and tubing finish off-gassing. A plastic taste that is just as strong after two weeks as it was on day one is worth checking against your install steps rather than waiting out further.

Why does my reverse osmosis water still taste like chlorine?

Chlorine removal is the job of the carbon stages in front of the membrane, not the membrane itself, and activated carbon gives no warning before it exhausts — it simply stops adsorbing once its capacity runs out. A chlorine taste usually means that cartridge is due, is undersized for a recent increase in your utility's dosing, or was installed past its rated volume. It is also a signal worth acting on quickly, since chlorine reaching your glass means it has also been reaching the membrane behind that filter.

Is a metallic taste in RO water dangerous?

If your system has a remineralization or alkaline stage, a metallic or mineral-forward taste — especially right after a filter change — is usually that stage reintroducing calcium and magnesium as designed, not a safety issue. A sharp metallic taste with no remineralization stage to explain it, or one that appears suddenly on a system that has tasted normal for months, is more likely a plumbing issue upstream of the RO unit and is worth mentioning to a plumber; it is general information here, not medical advice.

Why does my RO water taste stale after I've been away for a few days?

Water sitting unused in a storage tank or in the tubing between the membrane and the faucet loses dissolved gases and can pick up trace taste from the tank's rubber bladder over several days, which reads as flat or slightly plasticky. Running the faucet for ten to fifteen seconds before filling a glass clears that standing volume. If staleness does not clear after a couple of flushes, or gets worse, that points at a system overdue for a full sanitizing pass rather than simple staleness.
